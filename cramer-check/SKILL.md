# Cramer Check

Rules distilled (paraphrased, not quoted) from Jim Cramer, *How to Make Money in Any Market* (Simon & Schuster, 2025). Chapter refs in brackets so claims can be checked against the book. Sections marked **India adaptation** are Claude's mapping, not Cramer's.

## How to run a check
1. **Fetch fresh data first** (web search; never score from memory): latest-quarter revenue and EPS growth YoY, gross and operating margin trend, P/E vs sector peers and vs the index, 3–5y EPS CAGR (for PEG), debt vs operating cash flow, profit/loss history for 3 years, guidance vs estimates, any lawsuits or management churn, results date.
2. Run **Gate 1 (Eliminate)**. One fail = not eligible for the five slots (can sit on a watchlist).
3. Run **Gate 2 (Hero test)** and **Gate 3 (Valuation)**.
4. Output a scorecard: each rule → Pass / Borderline / Fail, the number behind it, and a confidence tag [Certain] / [Likely] / [Guessing]. Lead with the verdict, uncomfortable part first.
5. End with Cramer's street test (three reasons to own it, or why it fails) and the **sell triggers** to watch next quarter.

## Portfolio structure [Ch 7]
- 50% passive index fund + 50% active across **five** individual stocks + **one non-stock hedge**. No more than ~6 individual holdings: more becomes a homemade mutual fund and too hard to keep up with.
- Index: S&P 500 by default. **Under 30 → Nasdaq-100** (higher growth/risk); after 30, keep it and send new money to the S&P 500. Only add bonds late: ~20% at 70, ~40% at 80.
- Hedge: gold at 5–10% of the portfolio (GLD, or a disciplined miner in a safe jurisdiction, e.g. Agnico Eagle, Newmont). Bitcoin (ideally via ETF) is an acceptable secondary hedge; gold has been the better safe haven.
- Cash: only a few months of bills, plus dry powder for sell-offs.
- Contribute monthly to both sides; the ratio is flexible but don't starve either side. Example given: ~$250 index + ~$50 per stock + ~$50 hedge.
- Growth vs dividend mix: young = 4 of 5 slots growth; nearing retirement = 2 of 5; late = mostly dividend payers.
- Use a tax-advantaged account first (US: Roth IRA preferred).
- **India adaptation** [Likely]: Nifty 50 index fund as the S&P analogue; Nasdaq-100 via an Indian ETF/FoF or LRS for the under-30 rule; gold ETF (e.g. GOLDBEES) for the hedge; no Roth equivalent, so mind STCG/LTCG on switches and keep trading rare.

## Gate 1 — Eliminate (stocks the book rejects) [Ch 8]
1. **Deep cyclicals**: earnings swing with the economy. Materials (steel, copper, chemicals, paper), full-price retail, discretionary entertainment, **homebuilders/real estate developers**. These are "two-way" stocks you'd have to trade. Exception only when management has demonstrably converted the business to secular growth (Honeywell under Cote, GE under Culp) [Ch 12].
2. **Financials with credit or rate risk**: banks, insurers, lenders (India: NBFCs too). Reasons: regulation caps returns, first crushed in downturns and inflation scares, can vanish overnight. **Exception [Ch 16]**: low-credit-risk financial plumbing — payment processors/networks (Visa, Mastercard, Fiserv, PayPal, Block), asset managers (BlackRock), custodians (BNY).
3. **Concept companies without earnings**: losing money for 3 years running = no, unless it has a much-loved product that clearly scales (Netflix/Amazon/Spotify/Tesla type). Loss-makers must pass the Rule of 40 (Gate 3).
4. **"LSD" growers dressed as safety**: low-single-digit growth packaged food, beverages, staples. Only named exception: Procter & Gamble as one lower-risk slot for older investors. Growth is the only real safety.
5. **High fixed-cost businesses**: **autos** (value traps — low P/E because earnings keep collapsing), airlines, most transport (trucking, freight, rail), debt-laden department stores and entertainment.
6. **Suffocating debt** [Ch 12]: can it service debt from cash flow if rates spike? Debt is fine when easily serviced (Apple). Borrowing to survive (AMC) = out.
7. **Not investing** [Ch 4, 12]: meme/short-squeeze plays, 0DTE options, leveraged daily ETFs, thematic ETFs launched after a fad peaks, tobacco (Cramer won't recommend it).

## Gate 2 — Hero test: secular growth [Ch 4, 9, 11, 12]
- **Observability + curiosity**: you can see what it does and explain how it makes money to someone else.
- **Edge**: something you know from work or daily life; otherwise it must be a well-known best-of-breed. Never buy on a magazine tip or gut feel alone.
- **Best of breed** in a sector that survives calamities; grows regardless of the economy.
- **The troika**: strong growth prospects, great management, big gross margins. **Gross margin direction** is the key durability signal (Coke's steadier ~60% vs Pepsi's lower, choppier margins earns a higher multiple).
- **Recession test**: check sales and earnings through 2008–09, COVID 2020, and the 2022 rate-hike cycle. Barely dented = good.
- **Scale**: can it go regional → national → dominant? Profitable but no scale = don't waste research time. Does management have the skill to scale?
- **Beat-and-raise record**: consistently beats sales and earnings and raises guidance. An average stock wastes a precious slot; the index already gives you average.
- **Earnings quality**: operating cash flow and free cash flow back the profits; watch share-based comp hiding real costs; clean beats, no one-offs.
- **Total Opportunity Value** (Cramer's term): if everything goes right, can the company be several times bigger (Amazon beyond books, Lilly beyond diabetes)? If you can't imagine a big gain, don't own it.
- **Catalysts that expand the multiple**: new strong CEO (judge on the first call; watch the stock's first ~90 days), activist pressure, break-ups/spin-offs where parts are worth more than the whole, pivots from cyclical to secular (industrials moving into data-centre power).
- **Dividends**: a plus, especially growing ones (aristocrats 25+ yrs, kings 50+). Always reinvest.

## Gate 3 — Valuation [Ch 10, 11, 16]
- Share price alone means nothing; compare **P/E ("the M")**.
- Compare M with the index (S&P 500 ~19 historically, ~23 mid-2025) and with sector peers. Growing ~2x the market can justify roughly ~2x the market M.
- A high M signals confidence, not automatically "expensive"; a low M is usually cheap for a reason (Ford at 6 vs Ferrari at 52 — Ferrari won). Accept the market's current judgment; your edge is a better view of the future.
- **PEG** = P/E ÷ growth rate. Check before every buy; S&P ~2 as reference.
- Fast growers: judge on out-year estimates. Slow growers: near-term estimates.
- Beware **M expansion without earnings growth** (fads; usually ends with insider selling or a big secondary issue).
- **Rule of 40** for loss-makers and young tech: revenue growth % + profit margin % (EBITDA, operating or FCF margin; subtract if negative) ≥ 40 = real; below 40 = avoid for now.

## Buying [Ch 7, 8, 11, 13, Conclusion]
- Research before buying; be able to give **three reasons** you own it.
- **Don't invest all at once.** Wait for a price break: analyst downgrades on transient issues, macro or tariff sell-offs.
- Don't play the pre-earnings game; act only after the CFO's forecast on the call.
- Buy more on market-driven dips (pull next month's contribution forward) if the company itself is fine.
- Expect your first pick to be wrong; size so that's survivable.

## Holding — the homework [Ch 6, 9, 13, 14, 15]
- ~1 hour per stock each quarter at results (~4 hrs/yr/stock). Use AI to summarise calls, then check the nuance yourself. Set news alerts.
- Price drivers: ~50% macro, ~25% sector, ~25% company; over the long run the company 25% wins.
- Is the stock moving alone or with its group? Big institutional orders can create fake declines worth buying.
- **Calls**: the CFO's forecast at the end of prepared remarks matters most. Listen for organic growth vs price-led vs acquired; volume vs pricing (discounting = margin pressure); mix; the industry KPI; intra-quarter cadence; outlook; repeated "challenging/volatile" wording as a yellow flag.
- **Income statement**: sales growth, gross margin direction, SG&A % of sales trend, R&D maintained, one-offs behind a beat.
- **Balance sheet**: receivable or inventory spikes, short-term debt vs cash, long-term debt vs liquid assets.
- **Cash flow**: operating CF growth; FCF = OCF − capex; share-based comp.
- Check Reddit so you know whether a stock is caught in a meme fight.

## Selling [Ch 11, 20]
1. Beat but no raise → likely down. Meeting estimates → likely down or flat. A miss → downgrades; bail unless the whole sector is hit.
2. Falling gross margins on a high-M stock → expect it to be slaughtered even on a beat.
3. Outsized, immediate collapse you can't explain → big holders know more; sell, don't rationalise. A stock that doesn't bounce after a shellacking is another warning.
4. Don't make excuses for management when the customer base shrinks and they don't respond. Reputation won't save a stock.
5. Management turmoil or public infighting → sell, even for a great franchise.
6. Mounting product-liability lawsuits with plaintiff wins → sell; ignore management and analyst reassurances.
7. Never sell in anger; if the core business is sound, wait for a calmer moment or a bounce.
8. Diversification is not an excuse to keep a loser; the index side is your diversification.
9. Ignore billionaire doom pundits, Fed-watching, and inverted-yield-curve alarms.
- Otherwise: **don't sell.** Sell-offs are rain for the garden; trying to dodge them means missing the few days a year when most gains are made.

## The book's own sector map, mid-2025 (pattern-matching only; re-verify) [Ch 16–19]
- Low-credit-risk fintech and asset managers (Visa, Mastercard, BlackRock, BNY).
- Value dining (Texas Roadhouse, Brinker, Dutch Bros).
- Off-price retail (TJX).
- The power shortage (GE Vernova; natural gas as bridge fuel, e.g. Coterra). Skeptical of profitless nuclear builders, wind and near-term solar.
- Blockbuster drugs (Eli Lilly, Vertex).
- Subscription models (Costco, Amazon, Netflix, Spotify).
- Small-business infrastructure (Intuit, Paychex, Cintas, Shopify).
- Cybersecurity (Palo Alto Networks, CrowdStrike).
- Aerospace (GE Aerospace; Boeing as an early turnaround).
- Early tech screened by the Rule of 40 (ServiceNow, Salesforce, Palantir).
- Also covered: ten recent high-risk greats (Ch 17), eight income producers for older investors (Ch 18), and the Magnificent Seven (Ch 19).
- Cramer says he hated writing the stock-pick chapters because they can replace your own observation. Use them as examples, not a shopping list.

## India adaptation notes (Claude's mapping; verify each before use)
- Excluded by Gate 1: private and PSU banks, NBFCs, insurers; auto OEMs; airlines; real-estate developers; steel, cement, chemicals and other commodity cyclicals; low-growth FMCG; loss-makers failing the Rule of 40.
- Book-style categories to screen in India: capital-market plumbing with no credit risk (depositories, RTAs, AMCs, exchanges); value retail; power-equipment and grid suppliers tied to data-centre demand; aerospace and defence component makers; SME software and B2B subscriptions; telecom with pricing power; pharma with a genuine specialty or innovative pipeline.
- Watch for India-specific risks the book doesn't cover: promoter pledging, related-party transactions, government-ownership risk at PSUs, and SEBI/USFDA actions.
